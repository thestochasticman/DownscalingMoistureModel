# Few stations set amplitude, not pattern

The literature says that a few dozen sparse stations plus a handful of dense sites cannot *learn* a 10–100 m soil-moisture pattern, but they can *calibrate* one that is supplied from elsewhere. Dense-network studies show that a time-stable pattern carries roughly half to two-thirds of the within-pixel spatial variance on an ordinary day (per-date Nash–Sutcliffe 0.58–0.68 when every significant EOF is retained at Tarrawarra, Cache la Poudre and Satellite Station), that the pattern re-organises between a terrain-controlled wet state and a texture/aspect-controlled dry state over a narrow wetness band, and that terrain alone explains at most ~0.6 of the pattern when wet and ~0.1–0.2 when dry. Every downscaling family borrows its fine pattern from a carrier (radar backscatter, thermal evaporative efficiency, a DEM/soil/vegetation model, or a hyper-resolution land-surface model); when such products were tested against airborne 1 km maps at Yanco, all of them had spatial R² of about 0.1 and under-dispersed variance, and when station-trained machine-learning downscalers were benchmarked with spatial criteria their spatial skill was zero or negative despite good temporal statistics. The failure the research group has observed (pointwise models that do not preserve organisation or sub-pixel distribution) is therefore structural, not a tuning problem: any conditional-mean predictor fit to clustered points is a smoother, and random cross-validation on such points overstates transferability by factors of 3–4. What the evidence supports is a design in which SMIPS supplies the 1 km mean through a hard aggregation constraint, a low-parameter pattern model (two or more basis patterns whose weights depend on wetness, or a tiled process model) supplies organisation, Sentinel-1 or thermal fields are used as pattern *carriers* only where vegetation allows, the sparse stations fix dispersion (variance ratio and histogram) rather than shape, and validation reports SPAEF-type components and Crow-style "better than the coarse baseline" criteria against the SMAPEx airborne maps, the Yanco 3 km core site and cosmic-ray footprints rather than pooled station ubRMSE.

## The sub-pixel pattern is mostly static, half-predictable, and flips with wetness

The temporal-stability literature since Vachaud et al. (1985) establishes that the ranking of locations by wetness persists through time: the original 17-tube study found between-date Spearman rank correlations of **0.789–0.988** over 2.5 years ([Hu et al. 2013 review](https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/39868)). The relevant question for downscaling is how much of the instantaneous spatial variance that persistence explains, and the answer depends on which statistic is used. Mittelbach and Seneviratne decomposed spatial variance on the SwissSMEX network into a time-invariant term (variance of site means), an anomaly term, and their covariance; the time-invariant term contributed **50–160 % (mean 94 %)** of the spatial variance at any instant, while the spatial variance of anomalies was only **2–30 % (mean 9 %)** ([Mittelbach & Seneviratne 2012, HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/16/2169/2012/hess-16-2169-2012.pdf)). That figure flatters a static map because it is dominated by site-mean offsets. The more useful ceiling is the variance of the *pattern on a given date* explained by stable covariation: with every statistically significant EOF retained, the average per-date NSCE of reconstructed TDR patterns was **0.65 (Tarrawarra), 0.63 (Tarrawarra2), 0.58 (Cache la Poudre) and 0.68 (Satellite Station)**, so "roughly 0.60 of the variation is explained by significant patterns of covariation" and the rest behaves as noise ([Busch 2011, CSU thesis](https://mountainscholar.org/bitstream/10217/47254/1/Busch_colostate_0053N_10444.pdf)). Agricultural plots give a similar range (26–76 %, mean 47 %, Starr 2005, via [Hu et al. 2013](https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/39868)). A perfect static pattern therefore explains about half to two-thirds of within-pixel variance on an average day, less in dry periods; everything beyond that requires a time-varying component, and the same study found that the sites that best represent the long-term mean are not the sites that best represent the anomaly dynamics ([Mittelbach & Seneviratne 2012](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/16/2169/2012/hess-16-2169-2012.pdf)).

The pattern also re-organises. Grayson et al. defined the canonical "preferred states": a wet state in which lateral flow organises wetness along drainage lines (nonlocal control) and a dry state in which vertical fluxes make the pattern depend on soils, aspect and local convergence (local control); switching is rapid, transitional periods were **less than 15 % of the year** at Wagga Wagga, and the switch occurs at a relative saturation of about **0.6–0.8** ([Grayson et al. 1997, WRR](https://minerva-access.unimelb.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/61625f52-4f7c-5faa-9acc-92e540ca4787/content)). Transitional periods are the least rank-stable: on the semi-arid REMEDHUS network the least stable periods are the dry-to-wet recharge transitions, while stability is always higher when soils are dry ([Martínez-Fernández & Ceballos 2003](https://doi.org/10.2136/sssaj2003.1647)). At Wüstebach intense rain on dry topsoil abruptly raised the spatial standard deviation while barely changing the mean, because hydrophobic upslope soils and valley storage responded differently ([Rosenbaum et al. 2012, WRR](https://minerva-access.unimelb.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/a1893329-a068-5256-b6a3-dc6807ae2fc5/content)). The practical consequence is that a single standardised pattern with a scalar amplitude is defensible only in landscapes that stay in one state; in the Busch EOF analysis the second expansion coefficient at Tarrawarra changes sign with wetness and "acts to dampen out the dependence on PSRI" implied by the first pattern, which is exactly the valley/aspect switch expressed as two basis patterns with wetness-dependent weights ([Busch 2011](https://mountainscholar.org/bitstream/10217/47254/1/Busch_colostate_0053N_10444.pdf)).

### Terrain explains the pattern when wet, texture when dry, and neither beyond R² ≈ 0.6

Covariate models of the static pattern have a hard ceiling. At Tarrawarra (10.5 ha temperate pasture, >500 TDR points per date) combinations of the wetness index and a potential solar radiation index explain **up to 61 % of spatial variance in wet periods and up to 22 % in dry periods**, with R² of only 0.12–0.13 near saturation; the authors note that "it is rare to find that more than 50 % of the variation in soil moisture... is explained by the best performing terrain indices" and that "a major limitation on the predictive power of terrain indices is the degree of spatial organization present" ([Western et al. 1999, WRR](https://minerva-access.unimelb.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/b3c33685-c5fb-5296-9939-6aa8dbcfd0e5/content)). Attempting to predict the rank-stable site a priori from terrain fails outright (R² = 0.029 between wetness index and mean relative difference at Tarrawarra), and from LAI only weakly (R² = 0.18 at Louvain-la-Neuve) ([Teuling et al. 2006, HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/10/755/2006/hess-10-755-2006.pdf)). In flat or semi-arid landscapes the controls move to soil: soil type accounted for about 50 % of temporal stability at Walnut Gulch with topography less significant, and sand plus silt content explained 41–66 % of the MRD pattern on the Loess Plateau ([Joshi et al. 2011 summarising Cosh et al. 2008](https://vadosezone.tamu.edu/files/2015/01/2011_2.pdf); [Hu et al. 2013](https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/39868)). The Vanderlinden review of 157 MRD graphs from 37 publications concluded that seven candidate controls show "intertwined effects of controlling factors rather than single dominant factors" and that the evidence "remains contradictory or nonexistent" ([Vanderlinden et al. 2012, VZJ](https://doi.org/10.2136/vzj2011.0178)). Climate partitions these results cleanly: temperate catchments with relief show two states and a sharp switch, semi-arid sites sit in the dry state where texture dominates, which is the regime most relevant to much of the SMIPS domain outside the ranges.

Non-linear statistical models do not break the ceiling. On Tarrawarra, Satellite Station and Cache la Poudre TDR data, multiple linear regression, a spatial ANN and a mixture model reached highest median NSCE of only 0.29, 0.39 and 0.33 at Cache la Poudre, all performed best at moderate wetness, and best performance required **at least 50 % of points as training data** ([Coleman 2012, CSU dissertation](https://mountainscholar.org/bitstream/10217/67424/1/Coleman_colostate_0053A_11094.pdf)). When EOFs were regressed on terrain attributes and expansion coefficients on the spatial mean, within-catchment NSCE fell from 0.50–0.67 (true EOFs, predicted coefficients) to **0.14–0.35** (terrain-estimated EOFs); no consistent attribute set emerged across catchments and performance was "almost always worse" when a method calibrated in one catchment was applied to another ([Busch 2011](https://mountainscholar.org/bitstream/10217/47254/1/Busch_colostate_0053N_10444.pdf)). The process-based EMT model (a steady-state balance of infiltration, deep drainage, lateral flow and slope/aspect-modified ET with roughly 10–12 lumped constants) explains "on average between 8 % and 31 % of the spatial variation" per date from topography alone, and adding fine vegetation cover raises per-date NSCE at Cache la Poudre only from 0.080 to 0.134, while adding *interpolated* soil maps at Tarrawarra and Nerrigundah "decreases model performance" because "soil data must have greater spatial detail to be useful" ([Coleman 2012](https://mountainscholar.org/bitstream/10217/67424/1/Coleman_colostate_0053A_11094.pdf); [Ranney 2014, CSU thesis](https://dspace.library.colostate.edu/bitstreams/10217/88589/1/Ranney_colostate_0053N_12645.pdf)). These are the best Australian temperate-pasture benchmarks with full numbers (Tarrawarra, Nerrigundah, Satellite Station), and Cache la Poudre is the semi-arid analogue with the worst terrain skill.

### Variance tracks the mean on a texture-dependent hump, and scaling holds only piecewise

The amplitude side of a "coarse mean + pattern × amplitude" design has an empirical basis. From more than 36,000 ground samples, spatial standard deviation of surface moisture is convex-upward in the mean with maxima near **0.17 m³/m³ at 800 m and 0.19 at 50 km**, and standard deviation rises from **0.036 m³/m³ at 2.5 m extent to 0.071 at 50 km** with log-SD linear in log-extent from 16 m to 1.6 km ([Famiglietti et al. 2008, WRR](https://doi.org/10.1029/2006WR005804); [NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20080023356)). Stochastic unsaturated-flow theory reproduces the hump from the retention curve, with the SD peak between **0.17 and 0.23 m³/m³** across eleven USDA texture classes ([Vereecken et al. 2007, GRL](https://doi.org/10.1029/2007GL031813)), but the shape is not universal: semi-arid regions show only the rising limb and humid regions only the falling limb, as a consequence of the wilting-point and porosity bounds ([Lawrence & Hornberger 2007](https://doi.org/10.1029/2007GL031382)), with a texture-dependent threshold between field capacity and wilting point ([Pan & Peters-Lidard 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1752-1688.2007.00150.x)). The most widely reproduced empirical form is a negative-exponential CV–mean relation, found across 18 experiments at field to basin extents and differing between wetting and drying ([Choi et al. 2007, GRL](https://doi.org/10.1029/2006GL028247); [Choi & Jacobs 2010](https://doi.org/10.1002/hyp.7877)). At Tarrawarra variance ran from 5.3 (%v/v)² at mean 20.8 % to 24.1 (%v/v)² at 37.7 %, falling again near saturation ([Western et al. 1999](https://minerva-access.unimelb.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/b3c33685-c5fb-5296-9939-6aa8dbcfd0e5/content)). An amplitude model should therefore be fitted per site or texture class, with hysteresis as an accepted event-scale error.

Scaling across support is also only locally invariant. The scale-triplet result is that apparent variance increases with extent, decreases with support and is unaffected by spacing ([Western, Grayson & Blöschl 2002, Annu. Rev.](https://repository.geologyscience.ru/bitstreams/6a4648f6-7196-42c1-a63c-8915d551805b/download)). Power-law scaling of variance with extent holds from 16 m to 1.6 km in ground data, multifractal scale invariance holds from 0.8 to 25.6–51.2 km in aircraft data ([Mascaro et al. 2010, WRR](https://doi.org/10.1029/2009WR008855); [NTRS SMEX04](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20110015948)), and variability plateaus at CV ≈ 0.20 beyond about 10 km² in central Italy ([Brocca et al. 2012](https://iris.cnr.it/handle/20.500.14243/224908)); no study demonstrates a single exponent from 10 m to 36 km. The exponents are wetness-dependent: on SMEX02 fields the fourth-moment scaling slope was **−0.54 on a dry day versus −0.17 on a wet day**, simple scaling when wet and multiscaling when dry ([Das & Mohanty 2008](https://vadosezone.tamu.edu/files/2015/01/2008_21.pdf)), and in a Rur-catchment model 53 % of the variance of the scaling exponent was explained by LAI and antecedent precipitation ([Korres et al. 2013](https://www.tr32db.uni-koeln.de/data.php?dataID=693)). Field-scale correlation lengths are short, 78–307 m in Iowa fields and 35–60 m at Tarrawarra ([Joshi & Mohanty 2010](https://vadosezone.tamu.edu/files/2015/01/2010_12.pdf); [Western et al. 1998](https://faculty.washington.edu/edford/Western_1998.pdf)), which sets the sensor spacing needed to see a pattern at all. Hyper-resolution simulation suggests surface moisture is scale invariant from the satellite footprint down to 100 m, and that invariance has been used to calibrate a downscaler tested at 1 km against aircraft ([Mascaro, Ko & Vivoni 2019, Sci. Rep.](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-52650-3)), so a power-law variance target for 100 m is available as an audit even without 100 m truth.

## Every downscaling family borrows its pattern, and none has proven it below 1 km

The families differ in where the fine pattern originates, and that determines what a few stations can and cannot validate.

| Family | Source of 10–100 m pattern | Best documented skill | Pattern evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Radar (SMAP/Sentinel-1, S1 change detection, 100 m S1+S2) | Observed C-band backscatter | ubRMSE ~0.05 at 3 km; RMSE 0.06–0.07 at 1 km; 0.05–0.09 at 100 m on sparse crops | Spatial R² ~0.1 vs airborne 1 km at Yanco; vegetation unresolved below 1 km |
| Thermal/optical (DisPATCh, UCLA, ECOSTRESS 70 m) | LST-derived evaporative efficiency | R ~0.7 semi-arid summer, ~0 temperate winter; 100 m slope 0.2 vs in situ | Spatial R² < 0.2 vs airborne; variance under-represented |
| Station-trained ML (RF, XGBoost, CNN) | Covariate maps (NDVI, LST, DEM, texture) | ubRMSE 0.04–0.05 at stations | Qualitative only; Crow spatial criterion ≤ 0 when tested |
| Constrained super-resolution (climate literature) | Learned from dense gridded truth | Hard constraints improve RMSE; GANs restore spectra | Spectra, variograms, rank histograms; needs dense truth |
| Pattern models (EOF, EMT, EMT+VS, multifractal) | DEM/soil/vegetation via few parameters | Per-date NSCE 0.1–0.5 within catchment | Validated against dense TDR grids; transfer fails for EOF |
| Hyper-resolution LSM (HydroBlocks, SMAP-HB 30 m) | Tiled Noah-MP physics | R 0.71–0.77, KGE ~0.5 at 1,192 sites; spatial corr 0.66 between sites | No within-footprint or airborne pattern test |

### Radar and thermal carriers observe a pattern but fail the airborne spatial test

Radar is the only family whose 100 m–1 km pattern is physically observed rather than inferred. The SMAP/Sentinel-1 active–passive product reports "reasonable accuracy of ~0.05 m³/m³" at 3 km, but NSIDC states explicitly that the 1 km layer is "research quality, meaning they have not undergone validation" and that the Sentinel-1A/B combined revisit of 6–12 days degrades temporal sampling ([Das et al. 2019, RSE](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1016/j.rse.2019.111380?fields=title,abstract,year,venue,authors,externalIds); [NSIDC SPL2SMAP_S v3 user guide](https://nsidc.org/sites/default/files/spl2smap_s-v003-userguide_0.pdf)). The TU Wien 1 km change-detection product, now the Copernicus SSM 1 km, showed "high agreement over plains and agricultural areas, with low agreement over forests and strong topography" against a water-balance model, with positive growing-season biases ([Bauer-Marschallinger et al. 2019, TGRS](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1109/TGRS.2018.2858004?fields=title,abstract,year,venue,authors,externalIds)); a 167-station validation on three continents gave **intrinsic RMSE 0.07 m³/m³ and R 0.54**, with sparse-network representativeness adding 0.02 m³/m³ at one station per km² ([Balenzano et al. 2021](https://rsc4earth.de/publication/balenzano-sentinel-1-2021/)). At 100 m, Sentinel-1/2 change detection over irrigated Catalonia reached RMSE 0.059–0.087 m³/m³ only with NDVI masked outside 0.1–0.8, and "the RMSE can be seen to increase with NDVI" ([Gao et al. 2017, Sensors](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5621168)). The Danish test against cosmic-ray sensors found strong backscatter–CRNS correlation at 20–400 m at low-biomass sites but concluded that the SMAP/S1 algorithm "does not fully account for the vegetation dependency at sub-kilometre resolution", with 100 m downscaled R² of **0.13–0.53** and RMSE 0.04–0.13 at the agricultural site ([Meyer et al. 2022, HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/26/3337/2022/)). A knowledge-guided LSTM constrained by a water-cloud model on Sentinel-1 reached R up to 0.64 under four-fold spatial cross-validation at Australian field sites ([Yu, Filippi & Bishop 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00265)).

Thermal disaggregation inherits its pattern from an instantaneous LST–evaporation link that is physically valid only when LST is evaporation-controlled. DisPATCh over the AACES area of south-eastern Australia reached correlation ~0.7 with 1 km-aggregated ground data in summer but "approximately zero during the winter AACES, consistent with a weaker coupling between evaporation and surface soil moisture in temperate than in semi-arid climate" ([Merlin et al. 2012, TGRS](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1109/TGRS.2011.2175000?fields=title,abstract,year,venue)); at 100 m in Catalonia the regression slope against in situ was **0.2, rising to 0.5** after a nonlinear correction, with the linear approximation breaking down in irrigated areas ([Merlin et al. 2013, RSE](https://modarch.gsfc.nasa.gov/sci_team/pubs/abstract_new.php?id=08204)). A slope of 0.2 is a direct measure of fine-scale variance being under-represented. The ECOSTRESS 70 m soil-moisture layer is a linear regression of ST, NDVI and albedo onto GEOS-5 soil moisture, bias-corrected back to GEOS-5, so its pattern is entirely the regression's and no spatial validation was found ([NASA data.gov ECO_L3G_SM](https://data.nasa.gov/dataset/ecostress-gridded-downscaled-soil-moisture-instantaneous-l3-global-70-m-v002-96acf)).

The decisive test is the one airborne-reference intercomparison, at the Yanco SMAP pixel during SMAPEx-4/5. Radar-based, optical, radiometer-only and oversampling products were compared with PLMR airborne maps: "none of the downscaled products could capture the spatial variability of the PLMR soil moisture maps... products at 1 km had a lower spatial correlation than the products at 9 km, with R² values of ~0.1", all products "underestimated the variability", and the slightly better 9 km result was "an artefact of undertaking the evaluation at larger spatial scales where the high spatial variability is smoothed" ([Sabaghy et al. 2020, RSE](https://users.monash.edu.au/~jpwalker/papers/rse20-2.pdf)). The same paper concluded that "relying on in situ measurements alone is not appropriate for validation of spatially enhanced soil moisture maps" ([Monash record](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/comprehensive-analysis-of-alternative-downscaled-soil-moisture-pr/)). That is the strongest evidence available that temporal skill at points and spatial skill at the target scale are decoupled, and it was obtained in the same landscape the group is working in.

### Station-trained regressions are smoothers whose spatial skill is near zero when tested

The pointwise ML family reports convincing temporal statistics. A random-forest ensemble downscaling SMAP to 1 km over CONUS gave ubRMSE 0.047 (SCAN) and 0.040 (USCRN) and 0.035 at Little Washita, but its spatial evaluation is the statement that the map "closely follows the spatial heterogeneity of soil parameters such as vegetation cover... and topography" and "closely matches the spatial pattern of the original soil moisture data"; the authors also note the downscaled field is wetter than SMAP because it "is obtained based on the soil moisture at point scale" and that core-site stations "do not represent the soil moisture heterogeneity within" the pixel ([Abbaszadeh et al. 2019, WRR](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/45196/noaa_45196_DS1.pdf)). The multi-layer XGBoost product reports ubRMSE below 0.04 at 79 held-out sites and asserts that it "offer[s] spatial sub-grid heterogeneity" without a quantitative pattern test ([Karthikeyan & Mishra 2021](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1016/j.rse.2021.112706?fields=title,abstract,year,venue,authors,externalIds)); the Inner Mongolia and Iberian RF studies make the same qualitative claim ([Hu et al. 2020](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1016/j.jhydrol.2020.125360?fields=abstract); [Zhao et al. 2018](https://modarch.gsfc.nasa.gov/sci_team/pubs/abstract_new.php?id=28060)). Pattern realism asserted from resemblance to the predictors is circular: the pattern *is* the predictor pattern.

When spatial skill was actually diagnosed, it vanished. Using ParFlow-CLM 1 km simulations as a complete "playground", Leonarduzzi, Gruber and Maxwell showed that "existing biases in soil moisture station locations lead to spatial overfitting, resulting in poorer performance than... if those stations had been randomly located", and that these biases produce "unwarranted confidence in the improvement achieved with downscaling, as testing is carried out on stations in similar locations". Their SMAP downscaling benchmarked with the Crow et al. two-criteria test showed the temporal criterion "scattered closely around zero" and the spatial criterion "close to zero (or even slightly negative; XGBoost: −0.211, RF: −0.025)", improving only to 0.06–0.22 for low-elevation stations ([Leonarduzzi et al. 2026, EGUsphere](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-2956/)). The benchmark itself is a point-scale downscaling test of whether a strategy adds temporal accuracy and spatial skill "relative to its coarse-resolution SM baseline" ([Crow, Chen & Colliander 2022; EGU23 abstract](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU23/EGU23-7437.html)). Taken with Sabaghy, this shows that the soil-moisture ML literature has not closed the loop between pointwise skill and organisation at the target scale, and that the only time-varying input in these models is the coarse product itself, so the fine field is a conditional mean whose variance shrinks toward the covariate pattern.

### Climate downscaling already quantified the cure: hard aggregation layers and distribution-matching losses

The precipitation and wind downscaling community has measured the same symptom and tested remedies with dense gridded truth. An MSE-trained CNN for wind "exhibits strong low variance biases at fine scales", with standard-deviation biases of −0.127 to −0.204 m/s and spectral-power errors of 220–340 %, against −0.007 to −0.119 m/s and 3–32 % for a GAN; frequency separation (content loss on low frequencies, adversarial loss on high) addressed "the implicit tension between realization convergence and distributional matching" ([Annau, Cannon & Monahan 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2302.08720)). For wind and solar super-resolution the MSE-only CNN had lower pixel error (0.135 vs 0.157) yet lacked the k^−5/3 spectrum and semivariogram nugget that the adversarial model recovered ([Stengel et al. 2020, PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7382270)). Stochastic GAN downscaling of precipitation scored radially averaged log spectral distance of **4.88 dB versus 9.95 dB** for RainFARM and was evaluated with CRPS, rank histograms and FSS ([Harris et al. 2022, JAMES](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9788314)); a recurrent GAN with a discriminator that checks low/high-resolution consistency achieved rank-histogram KS statistics of 0.04–0.06, "close to the correct amount of variability", while the deterministic baseline won single-image metrics and lost spectral distance ([Leinonen et al. 2021](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2005.10374)).

Most transferable is the finding on constraints. Harder et al. added a renormalisation layer that guarantees the fine field averages back to the coarse input and found it "improv[es] their performance according to traditional metrics" (on ERA5 total column water at 4×, RMSE 0.582 vs 0.657 unconstrained), whereas soft penalties "fail[ed] to simultaneously reduce violations and maintain performance" ([Harder et al. 2023, JMLR](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v24/23-0158.html); [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2208.05424)). For soil moisture, a hard constraint tying 30–100 m cells to the SMIPS 1 km mean is thus a regulariser against the point-scale wet bias Abbaszadeh observed, not only a physical nicety. The soil-moisture uptake of these ideas is thin: Alemohammad et al. assumed "the scaling relationship between 36 and 9 km is similar to between 9 and 2.25 km" and checked water-balance conservation post hoc (1.5 % bias) rather than enforcing it ([Alemohammad et al. 2018, HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/22/5341/2018/)); PhySoilNet adds an evaporative-efficiency relation and saturation/residual bounds to the loss and improved R by 20 % and ubRMSE by 9.9 % over a data-only network, but with no aggregation or variogram term ([Xu et al. 2024](https://doaj.org/article/8dafc3719679479aaf8b47d408d5ff35)). Staged upsampling (DeepSD's three stacked 2× SRCNNs with elevation at each stage; Stengel's two-step LR→MR→HR; Harder's successive constraining) lets each stage be validated at an intermediate scale ([Vandal et al. 2017](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1703.03126)), and unpaired designs such as ClimAlign show training is possible without paired fine truth ([Groenke et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.04679)). No soil-moisture paper was found that reports a variogram or spectrum of a downscaled field against a dense reference at 30–100 m.

### Models as pattern generators: EOF and EMT with few parameters, HydroBlocks at 30 m

The explicit "coarse mean + static patterns × wetness-dependent amplitude" structure is the EOF downscaling family, in which the calibration dataset is decomposed into the spatial mean on each date, time-invariant patterns, and expansion coefficients that are then predicted from the spatial mean by cosine or segmented-linear functions ([Ranney 2014](https://dspace.library.colostate.edu/bitstreams/10217/88589/1/Ranney_colostate_0053N_12645.pdf)). Leave-date-out skill with true EOFs is NSCE 0.50/0.35/0.49/0.67 across the four test catchments; the method "performs well for the location where it is developed, but its performance degrades when applied to other catchments" ([Busch et al. 2012, HP](https://doi.org/10.1002/hyp.8363)). The EMT model makes amplitude wetness-dependent physically: each fine cell is a weighted average of process-specific indices (deep drainage, lateral flow, radiative and aerodynamic ET) with weights that are functions of the spatial mean, so that "the EMT model patterns show increasingly prominent wet areas in the valley bottoms as the spatial-average soil moisture increases", and the variance each index introduces is plotted explicitly against the mean ([Coleman 2012](https://mountainscholar.org/bitstream/10217/67424/1/Coleman_colostate_0053A_11094.pdf)). The model "outperforms the EOF method when few soil moisture observations are available for calibration" ([Werbylo & Niemann 2014, via Ranney 2014](https://dspace.library.colostate.edu/bitstreams/10217/88589/1/Ranney_colostate_0053N_12645.pdf)), was extended to multiple coarse cells where a fixed window per coarse cell "typically provides more accurate estimates" but creates edges while a shifting window fails to conserve the coarse mean (RMSE of the coarse-cell average 0.02–0.08 m³/m³) ([Hoehn 2016, CSU thesis](https://mountainscholar.org/bitstream/10217/178847/1/Hoehn_colostate_0053N_13884.pdf)), and the EMT+VS variant is patented ([US 11,808,752](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11808752)). Absolute skill is modest (per-date NSCE 0.1–0.5), every application calibrated against dense TDR grids, and no study validates a transferred-parameter pattern model against independent satellite-footprint data in more than one catchment; this is the central gap for an operational Sentinel-2-scale product.

The hyper-resolution alternative lets a land-surface model generate the pattern. HydroBlocks clusters 30 m land data into hydrologic response units and finds about 1,000 HRUs "sufficient to accurately approximate the fully distributed solution", with deficiencies "primarily attributed to parameter uncertainty" ([Chaney et al. 2016, HP](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/hydroblocks-a-field-scale-resolving-land-surface-model-for-applic/)). SMAP-HydroBlocks merges HydroBlocks-RTM brightness temperatures with SMAP L3E via cluster-based Bayesian weights and was evaluated at 1,192 sites: temporal R **0.71–0.77**, RMSE 0.07, KGE 0.53/0.48, and between-site spatial correlation **0.66 over CONUS but only 0.42 over the New York Mesonet and 0.54 over the Oklahoma Mesonet**, with the caveat that the spatial analysis includes training sites ([Vergopolan et al. 2021, Sci. Data](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8505542)). The precursor paper at four dense ARS watersheds reported correlations above 0.81 and KGE 0.56 ([Vergopolan et al. 2020](https://scholars.duke.edu/individual/pub1434950)). From this dataset the authors estimate that "on average 48 % and up to 80 % of the soil moisture spatial information is lost at the 1-km resolution", with variability "primarily driven by local variations in soil properties" ([Vergopolan et al. 2022, GRL](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU22/EGU22-352.html)). That number is model-derived, but it quantifies why 1 km products score so low against airborne maps and why point stations under-determine a 30 m field. No within-footprint or airborne validation of the SMAP-HB 30 m pattern was found.

## Point stations cannot validate a 100 m pattern; three kinds of reference can

Representativeness error is as large as the product accuracy target. Famiglietti's extent curve puts the standard deviation of surface moisture at roughly 0.04–0.06 m³/m³ within 100 m–1.6 km extents ([Famiglietti et al. 2008](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20080023356)), so a single 5 cm sensor misstates a 1 km mean by several hundredths plus a site bias of similar size. Against sparse single-point sites, SMAP L2 had ubRMSE 0.044 and R 0.675, rising to 0.765 once triple collocation removed point representativeness error ([Chen & Crow 2015, SMAP cal/val slides](https://smap.jpl.nasa.gov/system/internal_resources/details/original/358_321-Crow.pdf)). Even a designed core site's mean can be biased: with dense temporary networks as reference, the South Fork CVS scaling-function bias uncertainty was **0.034 m³/m³**, "more than 80 % as large as the entire 0.04 m³/m³ upper limit on overall ubRMSE allowed for SMAP" ([Whitcomb et al. 2020, JSTARS](https://plymsea.ac.uk/9087/1/09239894.pdf)). The Twente analysis recommends more than 30 independent measurements for a reliable reference and at least eight stations per footprint for 0.03 m³/m³ at 70 % confidence, finding that sparse networks "cannot reliably capture spatial heterogeneity required for satellite validation, particularly under dynamic conditions" ([van der Velde et al. 2021, HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/25/473/2021/)). Because representativeness error is comparable to the product target, ubRMSE at points cannot discriminate a pattern-correct 100 m product from a pattern-wrong one; anomaly correlation and TC-corrected metrics are more diagnostic, and the Crow review recommends temporal-stability site selection followed by triple collocation, which matched dense-network results within 0.007 m³/m³ using one sparse station per watershed ([Crow et al. 2012, Rev. Geophys.](https://doi.org/10.1029/2011RG000372)).

### The dense references that exist, and their spacing

Permanent core validation sites were built to replicate 3/9/36 km pixels with "10–30 (typically) permanent soil moisture measurement stations" over 10²–10³ km² ([Whitcomb et al. 2020](https://plymsea.ac.uk/9087/1/09239894.pdf)), i.e. kilometre spacing that tests the temporal behaviour of a 100 m product at each station but cannot resolve its pattern. Only a few networks have spacing below the 35–300 m correlation lengths reported above: TERENO SoilNet at Rollesbroich (40 ha, 189 locations, about 46 m spacing) and Wüstebach (27 ha, 150 locations, 900 sensors) ([Qu et al. 2016, ESSD](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/8/517/2016/); [Jonard, SMAP slides](https://smap.jpl.nasa.gov/files/smap2/0702h%20-%20Jonard%20-%20In_situ_sites_Rur.pdf)), SoilSCAPE/Tonzi with 150 nodes in two 3 km cells ([Moghaddam, SMAP slides](https://smap.jpl.nasa.gov/files/smap2/0702q%20-%20Moghaddam%20-%20In_situ_sites_SoilSCAPE.pdf)), NEON's five soil plots spaced up to 40 m at 47 sites ([NEON soil sensors](https://neonscience.org/resources/learning-hub/tutorials/soil-sensors-intro)), and the campaign grids. For Australia the campaign grids are the asset: SMAPEx sampled "six 3 km × 3 km focus areas with soil moisture measurements at 250 m spacing (Regional days) and... 50 m spacing (Target days)" with PLMR at 1 km and PLIS radar at 10–30 m across a 36 × 38 km SMAP pixel in five campaigns (2010–2015) ([Walker, SMAPEx overview](https://smap.jpl.nasa.gov/files/smap2/0302%20-%20Walker%20-%20SMAPEx_overview.pdf); [SMAPEx site](https://smapex.monash.edu/)). OzNet holds 38 ISMN stations with records to September 2021 ([ISMN OZNET](https://ismn.bafg.de/en/networks/?id=OZNET)), and a 2024 upscaling study using 28 Yanco sites identified two "dense" clusters of 12 sensors over 10 × 15 km² and 10 × 10 km², i.e. 2–4 km mean spacing even in the dense clusters; cross-cluster transfer gave r 0.6–0.8 versus 0.6–0.9 for random four-fold CV, and the authors called for "independent data (e.g., field campaign data)" before generalising ([Yu, Malone & Renzullo 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.05229v1)). Yanco is also the only Australian site NASA marks "Valid for 3 km and 9 km" in the SMAP/Sentinel-1 validation table, alongside Walnut Gulch, TxSON, Monte Buey, REMEDHUS and Valencia ([SMAP L2_SM_SP Release v3](https://smap.jpl.nasa.gov/system/internal_resources/details/original/522_L2_SM_SP_Release_Version3.pdf)).

Intermediate-scale instruments bridge the 100 m–1 km gap. Cosmic-ray neutron sensors average over a radius of **130–240 m** depending on humidity, moisture and vegetation, with 15–83 cm penetration ([Köhli et al. 2015, WRR](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/footprint-characteristics-revised-for-field-scale-soil-moisture-m/)). CosmOz operates 19 Australian stations with metadata updated in 2025 ([Hawdon et al. 2014](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/38492); [data.gov.au](https://www.data.gov.au/data/dataset/cosmoz-the-australian-cosmic-ray-soil-moisture-sensor-network/resource/d505e40c-a584-44e2-ae3d-a288b0c81c8e)); COSMOS-UK runs 51 sites each monitoring "a footprint of about 12 hectares" ([Cooper et al. 2021, ESSD](https://doaj.org/article/b4cecca13eaa40488514adffaac00e0a)). Six CRNPs on five continents sufficed to rank SMAP, SMOS, ASCAT and AMSR2 with triple collocation, with biomass correction flagged as a prerequisite ([Montzka et al. 2017](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/validation-of-spaceborne-and-modelled-surface-soil-moisture-produ/)). Comparing a 10–100 m product aggregated with the Köhli radial weighting to a CRNS tests its ~300 m mean, not its sub-100 m pattern, and the Meyer et al. 2022 numbers (R² ≤ 0.53 at 100 m in the best case) show what to expect. GPS interferometric reflectometry (~1,000 m² footprint, R² 0.76–0.90 for top-5 cm moisture) and fibre-optic DTS exist but have not been used to validate downscaled products ([Larson et al. 2008](https://radiometrics.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/larson_grl08.pdf)).

### Pattern metrics: SPAEF components, connectivity, Crow's criteria, and spatial cross-validation

A compact set of pattern metrics exists in distributed hydrology but has barely been applied to soil-moisture products. SPAEF = 1 − sqrt[(α−1)² + (β−1)² + (γ−1)²] combines Pearson correlation α, the ratio of coefficients of variation β and histogram intersection of z-scored patterns γ; it is bias-insensitive by design, and its authors show six calibrations of mHM with streamflow KGE 0.84–0.95 that produced different spatial patterns, concluding that "stand-alone metrics tend to fail to provide holistic pattern information" ([Koch, Demirel & Stisen 2018, GMD](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/11/1873/2018/)). Benchmarking against ~200 human judgements recommended fuzzy Kappa and EOF decomposition ([Koch, Jensen & Stisen 2015](https://pub.geus.dk/en/publications/toward-a-true-spatial-model-evaluation-in-distributed-hydrologica/)). Connectivity functions and indicator variograms distinguish connected from disconnected Tarrawarra patterns ([Western, Blöschl & Grayson 2001](https://people.eng.unimelb.edu.au/aww/tarrawarra/paperspage.html)), and a dispersion–distance measure with temporal clustering shows that dense-network patterns are "highly redundant" in time, so a few time series carry most dynamic information while spatial variability reflects organised state ([Mälicke et al. 2020, HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/24/2633/2020/)). Multi-objective pattern calibration raised soil-moisture pattern performance by 105 % at a 7 % cost to streamflow ([Dembélé et al. 2020, WRR](https://boris.unibe.ch/141969/)). No peer-reviewed study was found applying SPAEF to a downscaled soil-moisture product against a dense network, and per-day rank correlation, variance ratio and histogram overlap across a sparse station set is an adaptation of SPAEF to points, not an established metric. Variogram or connectivity comparison needs spacing finer than the correlation length, which only TERENO-type networks and campaign grids provide.

Evaluation design matters as much as the metric. Across 28,225 forest-biomass pixels, random k-fold CV gave **R² = 0.53 while spatial 44-fold CV gave 0.14**, buffered leave-one-out skill fell to near zero beyond 100 km, and a coordinates-only model matched the full covariate model, meaning "the model predicts a given pixel... based on its spatial—not optical and environmental—proximity to training pixels" ([Ploton et al. 2020, Nat. Commun.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7486894)). The area-of-applicability framework thresholds a dissimilarity index in importance-weighted predictor space so that "prediction error within the AOA is comparable to the cross-validation RMSE... while the cross-validation error does not apply outside the AOA" ([Meyer & Pebesma 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.07939)). Wadoux et al. counter that spatial CV "methods have no theoretical underpinning" and that unbiased map accuracy requires probability sampling, while conceding standard CV "was deficient with strongly clustered data" ([Wadoux et al. 2021](https://research.wur.nl/en/publications/spatial-cross-validation-is-not-the-right-way-to-evaluate-map-acc/)); the two camps agree on the symptom, and the constructive reconciliation is a probability-sampled validation set where one exists (the SMAPEx grids) plus AOA masking where it does not. SoMo.ml, an LSTM trained on >1,000 ISMN stations, used spatially grouped folds and was weakest in "Africa, Australia, and South America" for lack of training diversity ([O & Orth 2021, Sci. Data](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8275613)).

## Learning a field from tens of labels: the failure is structural and the fixes are known

Variance shrinkage of a pointwise regression is not a property of a particular learner. Ordinary kriging "present[s] a serious inherent drawback well known as the smoothing effect with decreased variation of estimates", which Yamamoto-type post-processing corrects for soil moisture ([Yang, Shang & Li 2010](https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/correcting-the-smoothing-effect-of-ordinary-kriging-estimates-in-/)); the kriged field's semivariogram has a smaller sill and larger range than the data, and the estimator under-predicts above the mean and over-predicts below it ([geostatistics supplement](https://www.sepm.org/files/8112mckinleydasupl.4w4j9uj7qgys3bn0.pdf)). Random forests, MSE-trained networks and regression kriging share the mechanism, and the shrinkage grows with the nugget-to-sill ratio and with the variance not explained by covariates, which with tens of stations is most of it. The soil-moisture-specific confirmation comes from the pattern-model side: when EMT+VS parameters were taken from global datasets instead of site calibration, only 5 of 16 parameters were estimable, "the use of global datasets decreased downscaling performance and the spatial variability of soil moisture was underestimated" ([Grieco 2017, CSU thesis](https://mountainscholar.org/handle/10217/185633)). Covariate-derived parameters under-disperse just as covariate-trained regressions do.

### Restoring variance by construction rather than by learning

Three families restore structure, and for tens of stations only the first two are usable without a dense fine-scale truth. Conditional simulation "is not subject to the smoothing associated with kriging (conceptually, the variation lost by kriging due to smoothing is added back)", reproducing histogram and variogram "at the cost of local accuracy reduction and increase in conditional bias" ([geostatistics supplement](https://www.sepm.org/files/8112mckinleydasupl.4w4j9uj7qgys3bn0.pdf)); sequential stochastic simulation on regression-kriging residuals generates equally probable realisations while keeping covariate trends ([Geologia Croatica 2015](https://doaj.org/article/8af593cd97374fecba8f8eddce5f27e9)). Area-to-point regression kriging honours the coarse pixel as a block constraint so that the fine map averages back to it; a geographically weighted version downscaled four 25 km products to 1 km with an average RMSE of 17.5 % ([Jin et al. 2018, Remote Sensing](https://research.wur.nl/en/publications/geographically-weighted-area-to-point-regression-kriging-for-spat/)). Combining covariate regression, a residual variogram fitted to stations, conditional simulation of residuals and the coarse-pixel block constraint yields a map that is simultaneously coarse-consistent, station-honouring and variogram-reproducing, which is the only route whose variance restoration is guaranteed rather than learned. The third family (adversarial, spectral, variogram-score and variogram-embedded losses) is well established for dense gridded targets ([Annau et al. 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2302.08720); [Harris et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02028); [ISGSR 2025 variogram-embedded network](https://rpsonline.com.sg/proceedings/isgsr2025/pdf/P152.pdf)), but no paper was found demonstrating any spatial loss when labels are tens of points, and none using SPAEF or its CV-ratio term as a differentiable loss. The computable analogues with sparse labels are a station-pair variogram score, histogram or quantile matching of station values (SPAEF's β and γ on the station subset), and the coarse-mean constraint; the frequency-separation idea transfers as "fit the low frequencies from SMIPS pointwise, let a distribution-matching objective supply the high frequencies".

### Coarse product as teacher, and the circularity nobody tests

Teacher–student arrangements that use a coarse model or satellite field as dense supervision are documented. Differentiable parameter learning trains a process model's parameter map against a gridded product (VIC against SMAP), "required only ~12.5 % of the training data to achieve similar performance" to evolutionary or regionalised calibration, and generalised better "across space and uncalibrated variables" ([Tsai et al. 2021, Nat. Commun.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.15751)); differentiable HBV-type models on 531 CAMELS basins reached median NSE 0.59 in spatial extrapolation versus 0.55 for an LSTM, with parameter fields that "maintain remarkably stable spatial patterns even in highly data-scarce scenarios" and guaranteed mass conservation ([Feng et al. 2023, HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/27/2357/2023/)). Pretraining an emulator on ecLand and fine-tuning on FLUXNET cut latent-heat RMSE by 30 % at independent towers ([EGUsphere 2026-3620](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-3620/)), and the single EA-LSTM trained across 531 basins (median NSE 0.742 versus 0.603 for basin-wise calibrated SAC-SMA) shows that pooling entities with static attributes beats per-site calibration ([Kratzert et al. 2019, HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/23/5089/2019/)). The guards against learning the teacher's own parameterisation are all evaluation-side: whole-region hold-out, scoring on variables the teacher never provided, and an independent in situ set after fine-tuning. No retrieved paper tests whether the fine-scale *pattern* is merely the teacher's DEM or soil-map parameterisation re-expressed, and this hazard is acute for SMIPS, whose own 1 km structure derives from covariate inputs. The training-side guard consistent with the dPL design is to learn only a low-dimensional parameter or residual field from the teacher and let physics or a variogram generate the pattern, then use the stations to re-inflate dispersion.

### Minimum data: 10–18 % of locations and three dates keep more than half the skill

The one explicit minimum-data study used conditioned Latin hypercube and stratified sampling to select monitoring locations and dates for calibrating EOF and EMT downscalers at Tarrawarra, Satellite Station and Cache la Poudre: "When the number of locations for soil moisture monitoring is reduced by 82–90 % and these locations are only monitored on 3 dates, the explanatory power of the downscaling methods frequently only reduces by less than 50 %", and strategic sampling "can substantially outperform random sampling when the number of samples is limited" ([Werbylo 2013, CSU thesis](https://mountainscholar.org/bitstream/10217/80296/1/Werbylo_colostate_0053N_11934.pdf)). Earlier sampling work found 3–32 independent samples suffice for a ±2 % field mean, that mild-slope positions are consistently time-stable while hilltops and steep slopes under-estimate the mean ([Jacobs et al. 2004](https://vadosezone.tamu.edu/files/2015/01/2004_1.pdf)), and that a few representative points reproduce areal means with R² > 0.88 ([Brocca et al. 2012](https://iris.cnr.it/handle/20.500.14243/224908)). The implication for OzNet is that whether the existing stations span the terrain and texture covariate space (an AOA-style check) determines whether they can calibrate a pattern model at all; the stations' job is primarily to set the dispersion of the pattern, not its shape. Mixed-effects reasoning makes the same point: predicting with fixed effects only prevents memorisation but under-disperses the map, whereas drawing unsampled site effects from their estimated variance "results in fully propagated uncertainty in predictions" ([spAbundance documentation](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/spAbundance/html/predict.NMix.html)), the mixed-model analogue of simulation versus kriging.

The operational baseline is SMAP L4, whose 3-hourly 9 km surface field has ubRMSD 0.041 m³/m³ against core sites and meets the 0.04 requirement only "when factoring in measurement and upscaling errors of the in situ data"; the report defines core sites as "accurate estimates at matching scales for a limited set of conditions" and sparse networks as "one point in the grid cell for a wide range of conditions" ([Reichle et al. 2023, L4_SM v7 assessment](https://gmao.gsfc.nasa.gov/pubs/docs/Reichle1465.pdf)). The finest formally validated US product is the SMAP/Sentinel-1 3 km layer; its release document states that "it is challenging and difficult to use measurements from one in situ site within a 3 km grid cell for validation" and defines a 3 km core site as needing at least three in situ sensors ([SMAP L2_SM_SP Release v3](https://smap.jpl.nasa.gov/system/internal_resources/details/original/522_L2_SM_SP_Release_Version3.pdf)); mission-level ubRMSE was about 0.05 at 3 km against 3 km core sites and 0.050 against sparse networks at both 3 and 1 km, with "limited availability of 3-km CVS data to support extensive validation" ([Colliander et al. 2022, JSTARS](https://digitalcommons.mtu.edu/michigantech-p/15512)). The USDA operational 1 km product behind Crop-CASMA, THySM, combines thermal inertia with a van Genuchten–Mualem soil term and reports overall ubRMSE 0.048, but NASA's own per-site table shows the 33 km SMAP Enhanced parent (0.032 / R 0.81) beating both THySM (0.049 / 0.69) and SMAP/Sentinel-1 1 km (0.059 / 0.61) at every core site ([Liu et al. 2022, JSTARS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20220005674); [Bindlish, WWAO 2024](https://wwao.jpl.nasa.gov/documents/91/WWAO-Annual-Meeting-Presentation-2024-Bindlish.pdf)). The fine products buy spatial detail at a measurable cost in point-matched temporal skill, and none validates pattern at 1 km.

The commercial 100 m claim is instructive as a cautionary case. Planet's VanderSat-derived product advertised 100 m daily soil moisture from passive microwave; its validation whitepaper acknowledges that "research on the spatial validation of satellite-derived SWC products is sparse", relied on a one-off 10 m experimental product and more than 1,100 hand-held samples in two Dutch fields, and found that "at least 7 measurements are needed to be within the satellite uncertainty [0.04 m³/m³]" per 100 m pixel ([Planet validation whitepaper](https://assets.planet.com/docs/Soil_Water_Content_Validation_Whitepaper.pdf)); the public specification now reads "the 20m and 100m products have been discontinued" and advertises 1 km, while a June 2025 20 m product carries no accuracy metrics ([Planet SWC tech spec](https://docs.planet.com/data/planetary-variables/soil-water-content/techspec/); [Planet product update](https://community.planet.com/product-updates/product-update-enhanced-soil-water-content-swc-and-land-surface-temperature-lst-for-agriculture-6227)). Europe's answer differs: the Copernicus SSM 1 km accepts station-weighted rho of 0.41 and ubRMSD 0.08 across 746 ISMN stations as the price of 1 km Sentinel-1 information, states that representativeness error "is assumed not to be negligible", and gets its best correlations (0.51) from the 132 COSMOS-UK cosmic-ray sensors ([CGLS SSM 1 km validation report, May 2026](https://land.copernicus.eu/en/technical-library/validation-report-update-2023-surface-soil-moisture-version-1/@@download/file)). The UK, German and Danish investment in cosmic-ray networks and clustered observatories (COSMOS-UK, TERENO SoilNet, HOBE's 30 stations designed for SMOS upscaling) is the only national-scale attempt to build references at the 100 m–1 km footprint ([Bircher et al. 2012, HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/16/1445/2012/)); an Australian effort has CosmOz's 19 sites as the corresponding tier. The US backbone of roughly 1,900 public stations ([NCSMMN Strategy 2021](https://www.drought.gov/sites/default/files/2025-10/NCSMMN-Strategy-Chapter-2.pdf)) is point-scale, and the only true spatial references are the SMAPVEX airborne campaigns, with PALS at ~1 km and UAVSAR at 6 m archived at NSIDC ([NASA CASEI SMAPVEX](https://impact.earthdata.nasa.gov/casei/campaign/SMAPVEX/); [SMAPVEX15 PALS data](https://data.nasa.gov/dataset/smapvex15-pals-soil-moisture-data-v001)). Structurally the Australian situation is the same three tiers (OzNet; Yanco 3 km core site; PLMR/PLIS airborne maps), thinner in the first and comparable in the third.

## What the evidence implies for the SMIPS-to-field design

The evidence argues for a pattern model with few free parameters and at least two basis patterns. The EMT (~10–12 constants) and EMT+VS (~16) models retain more than half their explanatory power with 10–18 % of locations and three dates, outperform EOF decomposition when observations are few, and keep the coarse mean when applied cell by cell ([Werbylo 2013](https://mountainscholar.org/bitstream/10217/80296/1/Werbylo_colostate_0053N_11934.pdf); [Hoehn 2016](https://mountainscholar.org/bitstream/10217/178847/1/Hoehn_colostate_0053N_13884.pdf)); a two-pattern structure (lateral/valley versus aspect/texture) with wetness-dependent weights is the minimum that reproduces the documented state switch ([Busch 2011](https://mountainscholar.org/bitstream/10217/47254/1/Busch_colostate_0053N_10444.pdf); [Grayson et al. 1997](https://minerva-access.unimelb.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/61625f52-4f7c-5faa-9acc-92e540ca4787/content)). The expected ceiling should be stated honestly: per-date NSCE of 0.1–0.5 within a calibrated catchment, roughly 0.6 for any static scheme, and much lower in the dry state unless texture is mapped at the target resolution, since interpolated soil maps *reduced* skill at Tarrawarra and Nerrigundah ([Ranney 2014](https://dspace.library.colostate.edu/bitstreams/10217/88589/1/Ranney_colostate_0053N_12645.pdf)). Given that SMIPS parameters would be transferred rather than calibrated at most sites, the Grieco result (global parameters under-disperse) means the stations' primary role is to fix the variance ratio and histogram, which they can do with tens of points, rather than the shape, which they cannot.

Scale consistency should be enforced as a hard constraint, not a penalty, and audited at an intermediate scale with a gridded reference rather than points. Hard aggregation layers improved RMSE while guaranteeing conservation where soft penalties traded one for the other ([Harder et al. 2023](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v24/23-0158.html)), and a shifting-window EMT lost the coarse mean by 0.02–0.08 m³/m³ ([Hoehn 2016](https://mountainscholar.org/bitstream/10217/178847/1/Hoehn_colostate_0053N_13884.pdf)). The "train 36→9 km, validate at 9 km, apply to 1 km" template exists for soil moisture only with temporal and water-balance checks ([Alemohammad et al. 2018](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/22/5341/2018/)), and the Yanco intercomparison warns that validating at a medium scale systematically flatters a method because variance is smoothed there ([Sabaghy et al. 2020](https://users.monash.edu.au/~jpwalker/papers/rse20-2.pdf)); any staged design therefore needs a variance check at the target scale, for which the power-law variance–resolution relation calibrated from hyper-resolution simulation ([Mascaro et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-52650-3)) and the wetness-dependent exponents ([Das & Mohanty 2008](https://vadosezone.tamu.edu/files/2015/01/2008_21.pdf)) give a target even without 100 m truth.

Sentinel-1 and thermal fields should be treated as pattern carriers under explicit conditions, not as truth. C-band backscatter correlates with CRNS moisture at 20–400 m only at low-biomass sites, 100 m retrievals need NDVI masking between 0.1 and 0.8, and the Sentinel-1 1 km product's rho of 0.41 against 746 stations with growing-season biases shows the carrier is credible on bare or sparse agricultural soils and in the surface layer only ([Meyer et al. 2022](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/26/3337/2022/); [Gao et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5621168); [CGLS report 2026](https://land.copernicus.eu/en/technical-library/validation-report-update-2023-surface-soil-moisture-version-1/@@download/file)). Thermal carriers hold in the semi-arid dry-down regime (R ~0.7) and collapse in temperate winter (~0) ([Merlin et al. 2012](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1109/TGRS.2011.2175000?fields=title,abstract,year,venue)), so the two carriers are complementary by season and cover type, and a sensible design uses each to estimate the *rank structure* of the sub-pixel pattern (SPAEF's α) within a 1 km cell on days it is valid, with the amplitude set by the SMIPS mean through the variance–mean curve and the coarse constraint. The 6–12 day Sentinel-1 revisit means the radar pattern is a slowly updated static-pattern estimate, consistent with the finding that patterns are "highly redundant" in time ([Mälicke et al. 2020](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/24/2633/2020/)), rather than a daily input.

Validation should report, per site and not pooled, the three SPAEF components on whatever spacing allows them, the Crow two-criteria test against the SMIPS 1 km baseline, anomaly correlation and TC-corrected metrics at the sparse stations, and leave-whole-cluster-out transfer as the headline generalisation test, with an AOA mask delimiting where the stations' covariate coverage supports prediction at all ([Koch et al. 2018](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/11/1873/2018/); [Crow et al. 2022](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU23/EGU23-7437.html); [Meyer & Pebesma 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.07939); [Yu, Malone & Renzullo 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.05229v1)). The datasets that make this possible are, in order of pattern fidelity: the SMAPEx-1 to -5 ground grids (50 m on target days, 250 m on regional days, six 3 × 3 km focus areas) with PLMR 1 km and PLIS 10–30 m airborne maps ([SMAPEx](https://smapex.monash.edu/)); the Tarrawarra and Nerrigundah TDR pattern datasets, which are public and already the benchmark for every pattern model above ([Tarrawarra data page](https://people.eng.unimelb.edu.au/aww/tarrawarra/tarrawarra.html)); the Yanco 3 km core-site upscaled reference recognised by NASA ([SMAP L2_SM_SP Release v3](https://smap.jpl.nasa.gov/system/internal_resources/details/original/522_L2_SM_SP_Release_Version3.pdf)); CosmOz footprints as 300 m means; and the OzNet stations as the sparse tier. For external comparison the 1 km aggregated SMAP-HydroBlocks release, the SMAPVEX15 PALS maps, TERENO SoilNet and NEON's 40 m plots are public and under-used, and the ParFlow-CLM "playground" approach of Leonarduzzi et al. offers a way to quantify station-location bias for OzNet before committing to a learner.

## Conclusion

The literature's combined message is that the sub-pixel soil-moisture pattern is a property of the landscape that must be supplied (by terrain, texture, vegetation, radar or a tiled model) and then *conditioned* on data, never a property that tens of stations can reveal. The achievable product is therefore not a 100 m soil-moisture map with 0.04 m³/m³ accuracy at every cell, which no product on any continent has demonstrated, but a 1 km-consistent field whose sub-pixel ranking is right roughly 60 % of the time in the wet state and less when dry, whose variance tracks the SMIPS mean along a per-texture hump, and whose uncertainty is honestly wider than the parent product's. Framing the deliverable as "SMIPS mean plus a calibrated, state-switching pattern with quantified dispersion" is what the evidence licenses; framing it as a learned high-resolution retrieval invites the Yanco result.

Two shifts follow for a group designing this. First, the scarce resource is not stations but *gridded references*, and the SMAPEx campaign grids, the Tarrawarra/Nerrigundah patterns and the Yanco 3 km core site give an Australian effort a better pattern-validation base than most US products ever had; the design should be built around them from the start rather than validated against them afterwards. Second, the open gap in the literature (no soil-moisture paper has trained with a spatial loss on tens of points, enforced a hard aggregation constraint to its parent product, and then verified sub-kilometre variance against airborne or dense data) is exactly the experiment this group is positioned to run, and publishing that test, whatever its outcome, would advance the field more than another station-validated 1 km map.
